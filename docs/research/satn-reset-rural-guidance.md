# Rural route-selection principles for the simplified SATN

Research for [issue #422](https://github.com/awjreynolds/agentic-satn-compiler/issues/422).

- Retrieved: 2026-09-06
- Scope: route-selection evidence for the simplified interurban SATN; no physical route selection and no detailed design.
- Status: research note and recommendations; not an accepted ADR.
- Existing decision context: [issue #15 resolution](https://github.com/awjreynolds/banes-satn/issues/15) already adopts ATE rural guidance as required Alignment Option evidence and requires multi-option appraisal, continuity, intervention archetypes, explicit rights and personal-safety unknowns.

## Conclusion

The official guidance supports a mixed rural network. A main road can be the only
direct and coherent connection between places, but its road class does not establish
safe or comfortable cycling. A traffic-free corridor can be quieter and more
inclusive, but it can bypass settlements, have weak natural surveillance, or lack
cycling rights. A country lane can form a useful strategic link only when its actual
speed, traffic volume, surface, visibility and management make mixed traffic suitable.

For this simplified SATN, the owner policy can make the A-road corridor a mandatory
candidate consideration for each genuine interurban connection, while allowing another
evidence-backed corridor to be selected when it gives the more coherent, direct,
safe, comfortable and usable connection. Prefer current/former NCN, existing
cycleway/Greenway and suitable bridleway or restricted-byway corridors as reusable
source evidence. Admit a B-road to the Strategic Main Network only to fill a genuine
missing interurban link after the preferred source corridors cannot provide a
credible continuous connection. Retain through-village sections when they carry
that interurban Main connection; keep village-only branches and local access as
Access Support.

That policy is a POC recommendation, not a rule stated by ATE or DfT. ATE expressly
says that the three rural route-creation families (busy roads, quiet mixed-traffic
lanes and motor-traffic-free routes) have different advantages and disadvantages and
that there is no fixed preference (ATE, §1.3, pp. 9–10). The recommendation adds the
user’s required corridor preference and B-road boundary while retaining route-level
evidence and officer choice.

## What ATE’s rural guide says

### Scope and relationship to LTN 1/20

ATE’s guide is for planning and designing walking, wheeling and cycling routes in
hamlets, villages, small market towns and the countryside between them. It can also
be used for comparable urban-fringe, country-park, canal-towpath and disused-railway
settings (ATE, §§1.1–1.2, p. 8). It says it complements LTN 1/20, builds on it, and
does not change its core principles (p. 8). It applies to all active-travel modes and
recommends considering horse riders where possible, especially where equestrian
routes already exist (p. 8).

The guide describes three principal route-creation families: routes along busy roads,
quiet country lanes and streets, and motor-traffic-free routes. It says all have
advantages and disadvantages and there is no set preference; a route may combine
types when each section, its transitions, crossings, junctions and wayfinding meet
the required standard (ATE, §1.3, pp. 9–10). This is the reason a corridor basis and
an intervention state must remain separate in the compiler.

### Network planning and option development

The Rural Delivery Roadmap starts with objectives, background/scoping, demand and
opportunity mapping, and a high-level network-planning exercise. Scoping can include
the existing movement network, PRoW and permissive routes, land uses, environmental
and historic features, local assets, designations, current and future behaviour and
development schemes (ATE, §3.2, pp. 19–20). ATE specifically asks for inclusive
engagement with disabled groups and with women and girls about safety and route
choice, including people who are not current users because present conditions deter
them (pp. 20–21).

For a network-based scheme, ATE says to consider alignments in the public highway,
along PRoW, new routes, or combinations of these. Each option should have a
feasibility assessment and identify missing data or knowledge requiring surveys or
stakeholder discussion (p. 22). ATE then describes multi-criteria analysis (MCA)
using practical considerations such as deliverability, cost, safety, attractiveness
and directness alongside policy, objectives and funding constraints (p. 22).

The MCA is delivery guidance, not a requirement to embed a universal weighted score
in this POC. ATE leaves criteria to the project team and says the RDR is flexible and
proportionate. The POC should preserve each evidence claim and its limitation rather
than collapse it into an unexplained route score.

### Coherence, directness, safety, comfort and attractiveness

ATE applies the five LTN 1/20 principles to all active-travel modes in rural areas
(§4.2, pp. 29–30):

- **Coherent:** a rural route may change between quiet lanes, highways and
  motor-traffic-free sections, but users must understand how the sections form a
  complete route; wayfinding is particularly important.
- **Direct:** routes should be as direct as possible for everyday work, education and
  public-transport journeys. Leisure trips can justify an off-highway or country-lane
  route where another principle is more important.
- **Safe:** actual and perceived safety both matter. Busy/high-speed corridors need
  protection and safe crossings. Social safety is expressly part of the principle.
- **Comfortable:** users need a maintained, sufficiently wide route with minimal
  effort, including people with limited mobility and people accompanying children.
- **Attractive:** landscape, views, local character and useful places to stop affect
  whether people choose rural active travel.

ATE’s gradient guidance says desirable values should be achieved for new structures,
but existing topography and quiet-lane alignments may make that infeasible. A steeper
route should be accepted only when scheme objectives and anticipated users are
consistent with it (ATE, §4.4, p. 31). Chapter 5 gives desirable and absolute
gradient lengths for cycling and accessibility guidance for walking and wheeling
(§5.5, pp. 49–50), but those are design values rather than a universal Main-route
selection cutoff.

### Traffic safety is evidence about conditions, not road-class safety

ATE says high-speed/high-volume motor traffic increases collision risk and deters
active travel; physical separation is essential in those environments (ATE, §4.5,
p. 33). Its Table 4-3 supersedes LTN 1/20 Figure 4.1 for urban and rural areas.
The table is based on speed and total flow, not the A/B road label. It notes, among
other conditions, that mixed walking/wheeling/cycling should be treated as an upper
limit around 1,000 vehicles per day or 100 vehicles per hour, with a much lower
interaction frequency preferred; where only cyclists/equestrians mix with motor
traffic at around 20 mph, flows up to around 2,500 vehicles per day may be possible
but should be lower (pp. 33–35). ATE also requires seasonal variation to be
considered and says actual speed data should be used where available.

For busy roads, the guide says the main-road corridor may provide the most direct
route between places and can be the only connection across a river, railway or
motorway. It is not usually suitable for mixed traffic, but can form an acceptable
route with separation, traffic management or a parallel route (ATE, §§6.1–6.3,
pp. 51–55). Separation improves actual and perceived safety, noise, wind, headlight
dazzle, air quality and route attractiveness; moving a path farther from the road can
also reduce perceived personal safety, so regular access/egress and lighting must be
considered (pp. 53–55).

ATE’s guidance supports considering the most direct main-road corridor for directness
and network continuity, while requiring route-level comparison rather than automatic
selection by road class. It does not mandate A-road consideration. The simplified
SATN’s mandatory A-road consideration and B-road exception are owner policy layered
onto that evidence. ATE also rejects a blanket B-road or quiet-lane safety assumption:
the candidate needs measured conditions and a credible intervention.

### Social safety, including dark everyday travel

ATE has a dedicated Personal Safety section (§4.7, pp. 40–41). It recommends:

- considering overlooking and routine activity;
- maximising visibility and providing sufficient escape routes, especially where
  rural routes have less passive surveillance than urban routes;
- avoiding narrow paths where possible;
- using seating and places to stop to encourage social interaction;
- maintaining vegetation and structures; and
- considering lighting, including after-dark work, school and care journeys.

ATE warns that lighting affects women and girls, older people, children and disabled
people, while dark-sky policy, biodiversity and hazards also matter (§4.4, pp. 31–32).
On traffic-free routes it repeats that cuttings, canal paths and long linear corridors
may have poor surveillance, few access points and fear-of-crime impacts, and says
social safety should be considered from the start (ATE, §§8.1–8.4, pp. 78–81).

The source does not say that lighting is always required or that unlit routes are
always unusable. It requires the choice and any lower degree of accessibility to be
recorded and justified against objectives, anticipated users, trip purpose and timing
(§4.8, p. 42). This supports a dark-use evidence field and an explicit unknown, not a
road-class or off-road safety proxy.

### Quiet lanes and mixed traffic

ATE describes country lanes as potentially useful over a wide area, but often less
direct and geometrically poorer than main roads (ATE, §§7.1–7.2, pp. 62–64).
Suitability depends on actual speed, traffic flow, surface quality, passing space,
visibility and maintenance. The guide recommends around 20 mph for shared use and
gives 1,000 vehicles per day or 100 per hour as an upper limit where pedestrians are
in the carriageway, with a much lower interaction frequency preferred (pp. 64–65).
It recommends regular opportunities to move aside, and says seasonal and HGV
conditions matter.

Traffic management can turn an unsuitable lane into a lower-traffic route. ATE covers
point closures/modal filters, access-only restrictions, one-way working, speed
management and physical treatments (§§7.3–7.5, pp. 65–73). It warns that
access-only restrictions can be hard to enforce and that signage alone may not reduce
through traffic (pp. 68–70). “Quiet Lane” designation/management and an
access-only motor-traffic restriction are therefore separate claims; neither label
proves safety or comfort.

Village and small-town streets are addressed separately in Part B (§§7.6–7.8,
pp. 74–76). Their local traffic, place function and access needs should not be used
as a substitute for an interurban corridor assessment.

### Motor-traffic-free routes, PRoW and former railways

ATE says motor-traffic-free routes can be important everyday links, especially where
roads between settlements are fast or high-volume. Existing corridors can often be
made usable at reasonable cost, but route rights, geometry, surface, gradient and
access controls may prevent some users from using them (ATE, §§8.1–8.4, pp. 78–81).
Routes must be integrated with the wider network; former railway and canal corridors
may bypass settlements, and their level separation can make access and surveillance
weak (p. 81).

ATE’s PRoW table gives the baseline user rights (Table 8-1, p. 78):

| Route basis | Walking/wheeling | Cycling | Horse riding | Horse-drawn vehicles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Public footpath | Yes | No | No | No |
| Public bridleway | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Restricted byway | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

ATE says public footpaths often need conversion or a parallel cycle track because
they may be unsurfaced, narrow, steep, gated or stepped (§8.6, pp. 82–83).
Bridleways already have the relevant access rights but commonly need surface and
width improvements; ATE recommends aiming for 3 m on surfaced bridleways and
restricted byways (§8.7, p. 84). Where a new traffic-free right links an existing
equestrian network, ATE says it should normally be a bridleway or byway where safe
and appropriate (§4.6, p. 39).

Former railways have gentle gradients and can provide direct, safe and attractive
connections, but they may bypass settlements, require new access points, be in
private ownership or need linking sections away from the old alignment (ATE, §8.11,
pp. 91–92). A former railway or former NCN label is therefore corridor evidence,
not proof of current cycling rights, condition, continuity or social safety.

Access controls should be presumed unnecessary unless essential and justified. Where
needed, they must be accessible to legitimate users; staggered barriers and controls
that require dismounting are specifically discouraged (ATE, §8.16, pp. 100–106;
LTN 1/20, §§8.3.1–8.3.7, pp. 85–86).

### Land, rights and the existing/proposed boundary

ATE’s route-creation appendix distinguishes dedication as a permanent right,
permissive paths, express dedication and modification of an existing right
(Appendix A, pp. 225–226). A permissive route may be quick and useful for a trial,
but its public rights may be withdrawn. Appendix B says that land acquisition,
planning permission, PRoW orders, cycle-track orders and environmental/heritage
consents may be required (pp. 227–229).

LTN 1/20 gives the same legal distinction: a footpath is on-foot-only, a bridleway
has rights on foot, horseback and bicycle, a restricted byway admits non-motor
transport, and a BOAT admits motor traffic as well (Appendix C, printed pp. 185–186;
PDF pp. 177–178). It also says there is no general statutory right to cycle on a
towpath, and cautions that permissive licences can be withdrawn at short notice;
where possible, higher-level rights such as a cycle track, restricted byway or
bridleway are preferred (printed pp. 185, 189–190; PDF pp. 177, 181–182).

The compiler should therefore retain `Alignment Basis` (for example,
`current-ncn`, `former-ncn`, `public-bridleway`, `cycle-track`, `former-railway`,
`a-road`, `b-road`) separately from `Intervention State` (`existing-provision`,
`upgrade-required`, `proposed-new-link`, `unresolved-gap`). Use
`existing-provision` to record an observed or mapped existing asset before a full
suitability appraisal. It must not imply that the asset is selected, safe, accessible
to every intended user or in good condition. Record surface, width, continuity, land
security, rights and after-dark usability as separate assessed fields, with known
limitations and unknowns visible; legal or permissive access does not establish those
qualities.

## What LTN 1/20 adds or sharpens

ATE is the controlling rural extension for this question, but LTN 1/20 supplies the
underlying cycle-network baseline:

1. **Network purpose.** A network plan connects origins and destinations and should
   provide on- and/or off-carriageway routes suitable for all abilities. LTN 1/20
   recognises primary, secondary, local-access, and long-distance/leisure route
   functions, and says multiple elements can form one integrated network (§§3.1 and
   3.4, pp. 22–25).
2. **Existing behaviour is incomplete evidence.** Fear of current conditions or
   inaccessible routes can mean potential users are absent from observed travel
   patterns (§3.2.3, p. 23). This supports treating existing use as evidence, not a
   demand ceiling.
3. **Main roads and directness.** Main roads are often the only direct, coherent
   route between places; adequately safe, attractive and comfortable facilities along
   them are crucial to network coherence (§4.2.5, p. 30). Routes can change type, but
   the connection must be obvious (§4.2.6). Directness is distance and time, and
   should normally be at least as good as the private-motor route (§§4.2.7–4.2.8,
   pp. 30–31).
4. **Safety is perceived as well as physical.** LTN 1/20 says remote routes may
   have crime and personal-security risks; frequent access points, lighting, removal
   of hiding places and passive surveillance can reduce them (§4.2.9–4.2.13, p. 31).
   A road-safety audit covers safety only; a mitigation can worsen directness or
   comfort and design decisions should record that tradeoff (§4.5.8–4.5.11,
   pp. 36–37).
5. **Rural traffic protection.** LTN 1/20 says busier major roads are usually the
   most direct routes between key attractors and generally need protected space;
   the same separation requirement applies in rural areas (§§6.1.1–6.1.8,
   pp. 50–51). ATE’s Table 4-3 is the later rural/urban replacement for the LTN
   figure, so the POC should cite ATE when interpreting rural traffic conditions.
6. **Shared use and continuity.** LTN 1/20 allows shared use alongside interurban
   or arterial roads where pedestrian demand is low, and where a short shared-use
   section is needed to achieve continuity (§6.5.6, pp. 67–68). This is a continuity
   option, not proof that shared use is the preferred design everywhere.
7. **Traffic-free integration.** Off-highway routes can be essential everyday links,
   but need high-quality surfaces, accessibility, lighting where relevant, maintenance,
   and clear links to adjacent highways (§§8.1–8.8, pp. 83–88). LTN 1/20 also
   requires a general presumption against restrictive access controls unless a
   persistent significant problem justifies them (§8.3, p. 85).

## Relevant case-study evidence

These examples illustrate the principles; they do not rank route types or establish a
national selection rule.

- **A27 East of Lewes (ATE case studies, pp. 26–35).** The A27 was the only
  vehicle, cycle and pedestrian east-west route between Lewes and Polegate. The
  selected 13 km route runs beside the A27 as a 3 m sealed, protected shared path,
  connects to existing PRoW and NCN 2, and was established as a bridleway. The
  route is direct, but junction/crossing protection, land acquisition, personal
  security and dark-sky constraints were part of the design. This is strong evidence
  for “A-road mandatory consideration, selection conditional on provision”, not for
  selecting every A-road section.
- **St Peter’s Greenway (pp. 16–23).** A traffic-free 3 m asphalt path was chosen
  between developments because the alternatives included a frequently flooded river
  path and a slightly shorter 50 mph road route with no buffer. The route uses a
  formal PRoW plus permissive cycling agreement, solar studs and accessible gates.
  It demonstrates that an existing or shorter road line can lose to a more usable
  separated route, while rights, flooding, maintenance and after-dark access remain
  separate evidence claims.
- **Exeter Green Lanes (pp. 37–46).** “Green Lane” is a local physical description,
  not a legal category; the examples include roads, footpaths, bridleways and
  restricted byways. Modal filters and enforced restrictions reduced traffic enough
  for mixed-traffic routes, while signage-only restrictions did not. Several routes
  connect the existing or planned cycle network. This supports treating quiet-lane
  suitability as an intervention and evidence question.
- **Keswick to Threlkeld Trail (pp. 4–14).** The former railway provides the only
  off-road link between the communities, forms part of NCN 7, and gives a direct,
  gentle-gradient connection. Alternative hilly country-lane options were considered;
  the delivered 3 m asphalt route retained limited lighting, access and landscape
  tradeoffs. This supports former-railway preference where it preserves direct
  continuity, while showing that the corridor still needs rights, access and safety
  evidence.

## POC recommendations

1. **Candidate admission.** For each interurban Network Place connection, require an
   A-road corridor to be considered. Include available current/former NCN, existing
   cycleway/Greenway, bridleway/restricted-byway and other traffic-free corridors as
   preferred reusable evidence where they can form a credible connection. Consider a
   B-road for Main only when it fills a genuine missing interurban link and no
   preferred corridor can provide a credible continuous connection. Preserve rejected
   or unselected options as inspectable candidates.
2. **Village boundary.** Retain through-village sections when they carry the
   interurban Main connection. Keep village-only branches, local street treatment,
   school access and place-making in Access Support; they should not silently become
   the Main corridor decision or substitute for a through-village connection.
3. **Continuity and uncertainty.** Require each Alignment Option to expose a
   continuous, bidirectional corridor between its canonical endpoints wherever the
   physical geometry is known or proposed, with every change of basis and every
   crossing/transition represented. A proposed physical corridor can remain an
   option when its delivery, access or legal feasibility is uncertain; show those
   uncertainties rather than deleting it or treating it as an abstract network
   disconnect. If geometry itself is missing, publish `unresolved-gap` and do not
   invent a line as built.
4. **Evidence-led comparison.** Keep directness, gradient/effort, traffic exposure,
   crossings, surface/width, social safety, dark usability, accessibility, rights,
   land security, maintenance and intervention need as separate observations. ATE’s
   traffic and gradient values can inform an option audit, but do not become hidden
   universal cutoffs or a composite score.
5. **Traffic and social safety.** Record actual speed and flow where available,
   seasonal/HGV conditions, protection from traffic, sightlines, activity/overlooking,
   escape/access points, lighting, maintenance and user-group safety. Do not infer
   “safer” from A-road, B-road, NCN, bridleway or off-road labels. Treat unknown
   social safety and dark use as explicit evidence gaps.
6. **Observed assets and intervention state.** Keep observed or mapped existing assets
   distinct from assessed suitability. An existing cycleway, current/former NCN,
   bridleway, former railway or footpath can be shown as an existing corridor with
   its evidence source, without requiring a full quality, rights or safety survey
   first. Record those assessments separately and expose their limitations. Use
   `upgrade-required` when
   a known intervention is needed; use `proposed-new-link` or `unresolved-gap` for
   missing links or rights. Permissive access remains revocable and must not be
   presented as a permanent public right.
7. **Quiet-lane handling.** Keep Quiet Lane designation, traffic management,
   access-only restriction, speed, flow, surface and personal-safety claims separate.
   A quiet lane enters Main only where the evidence and any required intervention
   support the intended interurban role; otherwise it remains a candidate or Access
   Support.
8. **Selection record.** Retain the multi-option appraisal and the reason for the
   selected option, including why an A-road corridor was selected or not selected,
   why a B-road exception was needed, and which social-safety or rights evidence is
   still unresolved. Do not use an unexplained aggregate score to conceal the choice.

## Residual owner choices

The evidence supports the above policy boundaries. One real choice remains for the
owner when two continuous, legally plausible options both satisfy the baseline:

- Should the POC’s default tie-break prefer the reusable off-road/NCN/bridleway
  corridor, or the more direct protected A-road corridor, when the former has weaker
  passive surveillance or a materially longer journey and the latter needs more
  land/intervention? The guidance deliberately provides no universal route-type
  preference. Recommendation: retain both options and require an attributable
  qualitative officer choice using the separate evidence claims; do not invent a
  global weight.
- How should the POC expose unresolved after-dark social-safety evidence on a proposed
  strategic route? Recommendation: show the route with explicit unknowns and its
  intervention state, while reserving claims that it is built or safe for evidence.
  No universal survey gate is proposed; product status semantics remain an owner
  choice.
