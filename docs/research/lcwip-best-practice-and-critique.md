# LCWIP best practice, criticism and product implications

- Date: 24 July 2026
- Status: research note and proposed decision principles; **not an accepted ADR**
- Scope: English Local Cycling and Walking Infrastructure Plans (LCWIPs), strategic
  equivalents, and protected-landscape adaptations
- Purpose: inform future features for the council-portable Strategic Active Travel
  Network product
- Evidence labels:
  - **Evidence** — directly supported by a cited source
  - **Inference** — interpretation of cited evidence
  - **Recommendation** — proposed product or governance choice
  - **Practitioner assessment** — experience-based qualitative judgement supplied
    to this research; useful context, not an official ranking

## Executive findings

There is no official national league table of LCWIPs. Active Travel England (ATE)
publishes local-authority capability ratings, but those combine leadership, network
planning and delivery; they do not rank individual plans. No authority reached Level 4
in the 2025 ratings and 11 reached Level 3, the highest level then achieved
([ATE capability ratings 2025, pp. 8–11](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6939a16933c7ace9c4a41f8b/local-authority-active-travel-capability-ratings-2025.pdf)).
This note therefore uses **reasoned benchmark exemplars**, not a claim that any one plan
is officially “best”.

The strongest transferable patterns are:

1. a hierarchy from county/landscape network to local plan, route, section and scheme;
2. distinct evidence, priority, feasibility, consultation, adoption and delivery states;
3. progressive disclosure through summary, HTML, map, place/route pages and technical evidence;
4. traceable candidate selection, prioritisation, options and delivery;
5. separate walking, wheeling and cycling methods with inclusion evidence; and
6. consultation, review, monitoring and supersession as a living lifecycle.

The strongest recurring criticisms are:

- reports can be too long, technical or difficult to navigate;
- plans are easier to produce than to fund, design, maintain and deliver;
- route lines can imply feasibility or quality not established by evidence;
- ranking can hide moderation, funding availability or political choices;
- walking, rural access, maintenance and inclusion can be under-developed; and
- static PDFs obscure evidence lineage, changes and delivery status.

**Recommendation.** The product should not generate one monolithic “LCWIP PDF”. It
should maintain governed, versioned plan data and publish several synchronized views:
a short public overview, a searchable map, place and route pages, a delivery programme,
technical evidence, consultation dispositions and committee-ready exports.

## National baseline and benchmark rubric

### Current national requirements

**Evidence.** DfT's published technical guidance defines three key outputs:

1. a walking and cycling network plan identifying preferred routes and core zones;
2. a prioritised programme of infrastructure improvements; and
3. a report setting out the underlying analysis and narrative
   ([DfT LCWIP guidance, paragraphs 2.1–2.8](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5f32aa668fa8f57ac88dc9dc/cycling-walking-infrastructure-technical-guidance-document.pdf)).

Its six stages are scope, information gathering, cycling network planning, walking
network planning, prioritisation, and integration/application. The guidance says:

- the process should scale to local complexity and larger areas may be divided into
  sub-areas;
- walking and cycling need different planning methods;
- LCWIPs should reflect the needs of people with protected characteristics;
- route and area audits should identify required improvements;
- priorities should consider effectiveness, policy and deliverability; and
- plans should be reviewed when circumstances, policies, development or networks change.

**Evidence.** The same guidance distinguishes LCWIP work from feasibility studies,
detailed design and costing, scheme delivery, and scheme monitoring
(paragraphs 2.11–2.13). A strong LCWIP must expose this boundary rather than make a
mapped proposal look delivery-ready.

**Evidence.** The policy baseline is moving. The June 2026 third Cycling and Walking
Investment Strategy commits ATE to new LCWIP guidance with enhanced walking network
planning, and support for authorities to update and integrate plans with Local Transport
Plans by 2027
([CWIS3, “Summary of commitments”](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-third-cycling-and-walking-investment-strategy/active-travel-active-england-the-third-cycling-and-walking-investment-strategy-cwis3)).

**Recommendation.** Conformance must be bound to an attributable, versioned
**LCWIP Guidance Profile**. The product must not embed the 2017/2020 publication as a
timeless checklist.

### Rubric used in this note

An exemplar pattern is valuable when it demonstrates several of these properties:

| Dimension | Benchmark question |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | Are geography, mode, timescale, governance and cross-boundary relationships explicit? |
| Evidence | Are present and future trips, network conditions, barriers, policy and uncertainty visible? |
| Network | Are walking and cycling planned appropriately, with continuity and network density inspectable? |
| Audit | Can a reader trace a corridor to route/area evidence and required improvements? |
| Prioritisation | Are criteria, raw evidence, moderation and sensitivity visible rather than hidden in one score? |
| Delivery | Are aspiration, feasibility, design, funding, construction and completion distinct states? |
| Inclusion | Are walking, wheeling, disability, personal safety and unequal access treated as evidence, not slogans? |
| Engagement | Are representations, response, changes and unresolved objections traceable? |
| Publication | Can a non-specialist find their place, route, issue and next decision without reading the whole report? |
| Lifecycle | Are ownership, monitoring, review, supersession and changed priorities visible? |

The rubric deliberately does not award one aggregate score. Consistent with the
repository's **Criteria Section** vocabulary, weakness in one dimension must remain
visible rather than being averaged away.

## National Park and protected-landscape comparators

**Evidence.** ATE funded all 10 English National Park Authorities with £100,000 each to
develop an LCWIP or equivalent active-travel capability. The resulting work adapts a
method principally written for towns and cities to large rural geographies, visitor
demand, multiple highway authorities and protected landscapes.
([ATE National Parks funding](https://activetravelengland.gov.uk/funding/national-parks)).

**Inference.** These are closer comparators for the repository's rural
**Backbone-and-Access Network** than an urban LCWIP alone. They also expose requirements
that a council-wide product must not treat as edge cases.

**Evidence.** The Peak District adoption material says the published LCWIP guidance is
urban-oriented and that rural active-travel initiatives had consequently been
under-prioritised. Its approach centres multi-user trails, settlements and gateway
connections, while recognising that the National Park Authority is not the highway
authority and depends on highway authorities, landowners and delivery partners
([Peak District adoption report](https://democracy.peakdistrict.gov.uk/documents/s60351/Revised%2520Active%2520Travel%2520-%2520Report1.pdf)).

**Inference.** A National Park route cannot be represented only as a road centreline or
an authority-owned scheme. The product needs explicit delivery ownership, land interest,
legal access, maintenance responsibility and cross-authority dependencies.

**Evidence.** The draft Yorkshire Dales plan uses a 20 km buffer around the National
Park, considers gateway towns and public transport, and includes green lanes,
unclassified unsurfaced roads, byways, Public Rights of Way and horse riding. The draft
is 86 pages
([Yorkshire Dales draft Active Travel Plan](https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2025/06/Active-Travel-Plan-Draft-Final-1.pdf)).

**Inference.** A governed study area and the functional journey area may differ.
**Cross-Boundary Gateways** should connect to named **Gateway Destinations**, public
transport and relevant external networks rather than terminate at the administrative
boundary.

**Evidence.** Northumberland National Park uses four Gateway Towns and positions its
work as complementary to 12 town LCWIPs outside or around the Park
([Northumberland National Park consultation plan](https://haveyoursay.northumberland.gov.uk/transport/northumberland-national-park-active-travel-plan/user_uploads/nnp-active-travel-plan-public-consultation-1.pdf)).

**Inference.** This reinforces a federated plan model: a strategic or protected-landscape
network references local plans through stable gateways instead of duplicating every
local route and intervention.

**Evidence.** New Forest's work integrates protected-landscape and ecological
constraints, including Natural England engagement and Habitats Regulations Assessment
([New Forest access and LCWIP](https://www.newforestnpa.gov.uk/protecting/people/access-to-the-national-park/);
[official summary](https://www.newforestnpa.gov.uk/document/new-forest-lcwip-presentation/)).

**Inference.** Environmental designation, habitat evidence and consent dependencies
must not be reduced to a generic “deliverability” score. They need attributable evidence,
an **Explicit Unknown** where assessment is absent, and a separate feasibility state.

**Evidence.** Lake District work coordinates LCWIPs at the National Park edge and
first/last-mile links, including access from public transport
([Lake District Active Travel Network Plan](https://lakedistrict.gov.uk/protect/people-and-recreation/smarter-travel/active-travel/active-travel-network-plan-introduction/)).

**Inference.** Visitor and resident journeys must remain distinguishable. A route may be
strategically useful because it connects a station, bus interchange or Park gateway even
when resident commuting data alone would rank it weakly.

**Recommendation.** Introduce a versioned rural/protected-landscape Guidance Profile
covering resident/visitor demand, Gateway Destinations, public transport, Public Rights
of Way and legal status, user suitability, land and maintenance responsibility,
environmental/heritage consent, seasonality and cross-boundary ownership. It must extend,
not weaken, the **Baseline Evidence Gate**: route rights, surface, gradient and conflict
differ across walking, wheeling, cycling and equestrian use, and missing evidence must
remain explicit rather than being replaced by an urban proxy.

## Substantive criticisms and limits

### A plan can become a shelf document

**Evidence.** In evidence to Parliament, Sustrans described LCWIPs as a strong way to
make network and multi-year plans, then identified two major weaknesses: funding was
available to produce plans but not necessarily to implement their priorities, creating a
risk that plans would sit on shelves and undermine the process
([Transport Committee oral evidence, 23 January 2019, Q96](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/8893/html/)).

Later evidence also described long preparation times, dependence on limited officer
capacity and the need to assess implementation
([Transport Committee oral evidence, 1 May 2019, Q318 and Q327–330](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/9262/html/)).

**Recommendation.** Adoption must not be the terminal workflow state. Every priority
needs an owner, next decision, funding state, dependency record and dated status update.

### Public usability can fail even when the analysis is good

**Evidence.** Complexity, length, access and jargon recur in official consultation
records for Oxfordshire SATN, Milton Keynes, Brighton & Hove and Loughborough.

**Evidence.** Current Government Digital Service guidance says HTML is the most
accessible publishing format. PDFs can be harder to find, use and maintain, and often do
not work well with assistive technologies; new PDFs should be a last resort and have an
HTML version
([GDS, “Publishing accessible documents”, updated 9 October 2025](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/publishing-accessible-documents)).

**Recommendation.** Accessibility is not satisfied by producing a tagged PDF alone.
Publish semantic HTML first, with accessible map/list equivalence, stable links and
downloadable PDF as a derived committee or offline artifact.

### Strategic lines can be mistaken for feasible schemes

**Evidence.** DfT guidance explicitly puts feasibility, detailed design, detailed
costing and delivery outside the LCWIP method. Oxfordshire SATN and National Park plans
also retain land, environmental and partner caveats.

**Evidence.** York's adopted LCWIP identifies a top 12 from 68 strategic cycle routes,
but says its sample route audits and high-level concepts were undertaken on varied route
types “not necessarily the top priority routes”; the actual first delivery phase was
still to be determined using strategy, economic and deliverability factors
([York LCWIP, “Strategic Cycle Route Prioritisation” and “Route Audits”](https://democracy.york.gov.uk/documents/s180434/LCWIP%20Report%20Annex%20A.pdf)).

**Inference.** A map can communicate certainty more strongly than its underlying
evidence. A continuous line is especially liable to be read as an approved route, while
a high rank can precede route-specific audit or feasibility.

**Recommendation.** Keep **Network Validity**, **Benefit or Priority**,
**Feasibility**, **Consultation** and **Adoption** visibly independent. Never generate an
overall “LCWIP compliant” or “ready” status that collapses them. Publish audit coverage
and evidence gaps against each route rather than implying that sample audits cover the
ranked network.

### Quality can be diluted between plan and construction

**Evidence.** Kettering's committee report records consultation criticism of existing
facilities with variable widths, repeated give-way points and poor maintenance or
vegetation encroachment. It says future proposals must not be diluted as they pass
through design and should account for maintenance
([Kettering LCWIP committee report, paragraphs 7.5.11–7.5.12](https://northnorthants.moderngov.co.uk/documents/s19480/Kettering%20Local%20Cycling%20and%20Walking%20Infrastructure%20Plan%20LCWIP.pdf)).

**Recommendation.** Preserve outcome requirements and audit findings through later
scheme states. A delivered scheme should be testable against the need recorded in the
plan, not merely share its name or approximate corridor.

### Prioritisation is partly normative

**Evidence.** DfT proposes effectiveness, policy and deliverability considerations, not
one mandated formula. Oxfordshire SATN combined calculated scores with officer judgement
and the existing delivery pipeline. Funding availability often changes short-term
implementation order.

**Inference.** A single composite rank can hide value choices, circularly reward schemes
already in development and make uncertainty look objective.

**Recommendation.** The future **Prioritisation Pass** should retain separate
**Criteria Sections**, raw measures, weights or rules, sensitivity results, moderation
reasons and the identity of the accountable human decision. Backbone-Outward Assembly
order must never be presented as delivery priority.

### Consultation has limits

**Evidence.** Strong exemplars publish large response counts and consultation reports,
but raw participation is self-selecting. Official responses also show requests for wider
engagement, clearer impacts and more detail.

**Evidence.** York's adopted plan places wheelchair users and people with protected
characteristics at the top of its transport hierarchy, but the adoption minutes record
an apology that the York Disability Rights Forum had not been involved with the final
draft
([City of York Council decision and minutes](https://democracy.york.gov.uk/ieDecisionDetails.aspx?AIId=69253)).

**Inference.** Consultation is governed evidence about representations; it is not a
referendum, a representative sample by default, a feasibility test or **Adoption**.
Strong inclusive content and complete participation are also different claims.

**Recommendation.** Store representation coverage, method, affected features, response,
stage, change and unresolved issue, including named gaps in expected representation.
Keep Controlled Evidence out of public snapshots and publish an appropriately redacted
disposition.

### Rural evidence is structurally different

**Evidence.** National Park work identifies urban bias in the current method and depends
on gateways, visitor movements, Public Rights of Way, landowners, environmental
constraints and several delivery authorities.

**Recommendation.** Do not apply an unexplained urban demand threshold that erases
low-population but essential rural links. Demand, accessibility, network continuity,
visitor use and lack of alternatives should remain inspectable dimensions.

## Oxfordshire as a readability case

Oxfordshire is both a strong benchmark and a warning against equating completeness with
usability.

**Evidence.**

- Oxfordshire achieved ATE Level 3, the highest 2025 capability level.
- Its local-plan-plus-SATN hierarchy is unusually comprehensive.
- The SATN consultation response says the report was complex and that a simplified
  version should be published
  ([Oxfordshire SATN “You said, we did”](https://letstalk.oxfordshire.gov.uk/strategic-active-travel-network-satn-yswd)).
- Direct inspection of the official files for this research found:
  - SATN final report: 62 pages, A3 landscape, 8.8 MB, tagged PDF;
  - Thame final LCWIP: 96 pages, A3 landscape, 11.1 MB, untagged PDF; and
  - Eynsham LCWIP: 55 pages, A4, 5.2 MB, tagged PDF.

Official files:
[SATN](https://www.oxfordshire.gov.uk/sites/default/files/file/roads-and-transport-policies-and-plans/OxfordshireSATN.pdf),
[Thame](https://www.oxfordshire.gov.uk/sites/default/files/file/roads-and-transport/thamelcwipfinalreportlowres.pdf),
and
[Eynsham](https://www.oxfordshire.gov.uk/sites/default/files/file/roads-and-transport-policies-and-plans/EynshamLCWIP.pdf).

**Uncertainty.** File measurements describe publication burden. They do not establish
that “nobody reads” the plans, and no reliable readership analytics were found. The
stronger supported claim is that Oxfordshire itself received complexity feedback and
accepted the need for a simpler version.

**Inference.** Shortening the technical report alone would lose evidence. The better
answer is progressive disclosure from one governed model:

1. a two-page public overview;
2. a searchable map and place index;
3. route and scheme cards with status and next decision;
4. expandable evidence, audit, alternatives and consultation;
5. a technical method and source register; and
6. generated committee, funding and archival exports.

## Proposed product decision principles

These principles are recommendations for a future ADR. They are not yet repository
decisions.

1. **Data first, documents derived.** Store stable plan entities, evidence, decisions and
   lifecycle state; render reports from them.
2. **Progressive disclosure by default.** A reader starts with their place, route or
   decision and can descend to full technical evidence.
3. **HTML first.** The Inspectable Review Map and semantic HTML are the primary public
   artifact; PDF is a synchronized derivative.
4. **One fact, multiple views.** Maps, lists, route pages, programme tables and reports
   share stable identifiers and cannot drift.
5. **Separate scale and purpose.** Strategic, local, route, section and scheme entities
   are related but not interchangeable.
6. **Separate validity, priority and authority.** Network topology, benefit, feasibility,
   consultation and adoption remain distinct.
7. **Walking and wheeling are first-class.** Candidate Low-Traffic Areas and School
   Access Obligations do not substitute for Core Walking Zones, walking routes and
   audits.
8. **Rural is a governed profile, not an exception.** Gateways, Public Rights of Way,
   visitors, equestrians, land and protected landscapes receive explicit evidence rules.
9. **Prioritisation is explainable and reversible.** Raw evidence, criteria, moderation
   and sensitivity survive every ranked view.
10. **Adoption starts delivery tracking.** An adopted LCWIP Release remains live until
    superseded, with named ownership and review triggers.
11. **Unknown stays unknown.** Missing audit, land, ecological, legal or equality
    evidence cannot become a neutral score or implied permission.
12. **Guidance is versioned.** Every release records the LCWIP Guidance Profile it used,
    especially across the expected 2027 guidance change.

### Minimum useful route or scheme card

**Recommendation.** A public card should answer, without requiring the technical report:

- What place and journey need does this serve?
- Is it a **Community Connection**, Strategic Spine section, walking route or area
  improvement?
- What exists now, and what evidence supports that statement?
- What problem or required outcome was found?
- Which options were considered and why was one selected?
- Is the item valid as a network proposal, prioritised, feasible, consulted, adopted,
  funded, designed or delivered?
- What are the known land, legal, environmental, accessibility and maintenance
  constraints?
- Who owns the next action, when is it due, and what decision is required?
- What changed after engagement or a later release?

### Candidate publication architecture

```text
LCWIP Release
├── public overview
├── map and searchable place index
│   ├── strategic network
│   ├── local walking and cycling networks
│   └── gaps, constraints and delivery status
├── place pages
├── route and scheme cards
├── prioritised programme
├── consultation and equality record
├── delivery and monitoring dashboard
├── technical method and evidence register
└── generated PDF, GIS and machine-readable exports
```

This structure should reuse the current stable identifiers, **Evidence Lineage**,
**Criterion Status**, **Challenge Finding** and atomic publication discipline.

## Non-goals

The proposed product should not:

- declare one LCWIP “best” from an aggregate score;
- infer public support from consultation response counts;
- treat a mapped route as safe, feasible, funded or adopted;
- replace site audit, ecological assessment, land negotiation or detailed design;
- use Backbone-Outward Assembly order as Benefit or Priority;
- make an Agent Runtime the accountable prioritisation or adoption authority;
- expose Controlled Evidence or silently substitute absent rural data; or
- overwrite an adopted LCWIP Release when evidence or guidance changes.

## Confidence and evidence gaps

- **High confidence:** national outputs and stages, separate walking/cycling methods,
  guidance version risk, Oxfordshire's two-scale structure, and recurring usability and
  delivery concerns.
- **Medium-high confidence:** the selected authorities are strong feature-level
  benchmarks. They are not an exhaustive sample and do not establish overall plan
  quality.
- **Medium confidence:** National Park patterns are transferable to every rural council.
  Protected landscapes have distinctive visitor, planning and governance conditions.
- **Evidence gaps:** no common national document-quality assessment or comparable
  readership/accessibility testing was found; publication does not prove delivery or
  outcomes.

The decision-safe conclusion is therefore:

> Build a governed LCWIP workspace that can expose the depth of Oxfordshire-like
> evidence without imposing Oxfordshire-like document burden, and that connects every
> strategic proposal to local context, delivery state, accountable decisions and a
> versioned public explanation.
